The 2022 is the clearest mispricing on the ladder and it is not close — the next-best row is 16% behind it. It is a great+ year, it carries the joint-highest gate scores here (William Kelley 95–97, Burghound 94–96), and it is asking HK$5,330 while the merely very good 2011 and 2014 ask 5,460 and 6,120. The market has simply not repriced it yet.

You already hold three bottles at HK$5,800. That is 9% above today's cheapest, so this is not a correction of a bad buy — it is the same wine, still cheap, and the honest recommendation is to add rather than to admire. Where it is weak: single bottles only, no case anywhere near this price, and you will not open one before 2034.

In the glass

Look

Pale. Noticeably paler than most grand cru red Burgundy and paler than the domaine's own Clos de la Roche — a light translucent red that lets you see the stem of the glass through it.

⚠ Inferred from the site and the house method. Critics rarely describe colour and none of the fifteen notes here does.

Nose

Rose petal, red cherry and wild strawberry, with a green-tea and dried-herb edge from the whole bunches. Clos Saint-Denis has always been the scented one of the two Morey grand crus; Dujac's method pushes it further that way.

Palate

Light-bodied and smooth, with the tannin fine and arriving late — closer to silk than to grip. It tastes weightless for a grand cru, which is the point of the wine rather than a shortcoming, and it needs a decade before the stems fold in.

Palate fit — the best-matched wine Dujac makes

The land and the grower pull the same way, which is rare. Clos Saint-Denis is itself a Delicate & Perfumed site and Dujac is a Delicate & Perfumed grower, so the wine resolves at 69% to the single style you like most. Set against the domaine's Clos de la Roche — the same producer, the next vineyard along — the difference is stark: that cru is Firm & Structured land, the blend lands at only 33% Delicate & Perfumed, and the base palate fit falls from +2.3 to +0.9. Same producer, same village, and the wine suits you two and a half times better. If you are choosing between the two Dujac grand crus, this is the one.

Then the year moves the fit more than the wine does. Cool vintages take +2.6 — 2013, 2014 and 2021 — while the hot 2015, 2018 and 2020 give almost all of it back at +0.1. That is a 2.5-point swing from one vineyard, and it is why the exceptional 2015 lands bottom of this ladder while the merely very good 2014 does not.

The practical reading: 2021 is the pure palate pick and 2022 is the buy. The 2021 is a cool year with the top palate-adjusted score on the page (96.8) and a gate score of NM 94, but it is asking 5,900 against the 2022's 5,330 for a lesser vintage — and it is the one row with a materially cheaper case sitting behind it. Taste says 2021; the money says 2022.

What the ladder says, and what to distrust

The model tracks this market better than most. Correlating the two across the fourteen priced vintages, with the level divided out, gives r = +0.64 — the strongest reading on any ladder run so far, against +0.43 on Grivault and +0.47 on Trapet's Chapelle. The market really does pay for vintage quality on this wine: the median cheapest-buyable climbs cleanly from HK$5,900 in a very good year to 7,449 in a great+ one to 9,900 for the exceptional 2015. The percentages on this page are therefore worth more than usual.

But the market discriminates more than the model does, not less. The model's prices span 1.40× top to bottom; the actual asking prices span 1.93×. That is the opposite of the usual warning — here the framework is the conservative one, and the extremes of the ladder (the 2022 at the cheap end, the 2015 at the dear end) are further apart in reality than the model believes.

The August version of this report said the framework could not tell these two vineyards apart. That has since been fixed — and the fix overshot. When this ladder was first run the model priced Dujac's Clos Saint-Denis and his Clos de la Roche identically, and the report asked for a small step between them. A step went in. It was not small: the framework now asks a 33% premium for Clos Saint-Denis over the domaine's Clos de la Roche. Across the fourteen vintages where both were priced on the same day, the market pays a median of 7% — and on three of them, 2010, 2019 and 2021, Clos de la Roche was the dearer wine. The correction went in the right direction and roughly four times too far. Since a level error like this multiplies every row by the same number, the order of the ladder is unaffected but every "% cheaper" on the page is overstated — by close to a fifth. The 2022 is still the buy; it is not 28% cheap, it is more like 15%. The step wants revisiting, and this ladder is the instrument that should set it — one producer across two crus is the only measurement that isolates the vineyard.

What to distrust. Seven of the thirteen rows with a case pool have a cheaper case sitting behind the cheapest-buyable — worst on the 2021, where a case runs 19% under it. That is the Hong-Kong-first rule doing its job: those cheaper cases are in bond overseas, and if you are willing to handle in-bond yourself the whole ladder is cheaper than it reads here. The 2024 cannot be bought at all — it has a market value of HK$5,707 and no purchasable offer on either query, which for an unreleased vintage is normal, not a warning. The 2015 has no case offer anywhere on the ladder, and the 2010 rests on 8 offers with a single case, the thinnest row here — its eye-catching 69% gap to the next case is one listing, not a market. Finally, five of the fifteen rows lean on Stephen Tanzer, who leaves the Burgundy gate after the 2015 vintage, so critic coverage is thinner at the old end than the column makes it look.
