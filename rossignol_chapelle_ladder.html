Rossignol-Trapet is the other half of the Trapet family estate, split in 1990, and it sells at roughly two-thirds what Jean-Louis Trapet's wines fetch. Chapelle-Chambertin is the gentlest of the Gevrey grand crus — the framework calls the site itself Delicate & Perfumed, which is unusual for the commune — so this is the one wine of the three where the vineyard and the producer both point at the style you like. It resolves to 52% Delicate & Perfumed, the cleanest reading on any Trapet ladder. The 2019 is a great+ year, the cheapest bottle here, sold as a proper six-pack, with fifteen case offers standing behind it.

What this ladder was really for. Chapelle and Latricières sit at the same cru level (S−), so putting this ladder beside Trapet's Latricières isolates the producer gap the way the last pair isolated the cru gap. Across the nine vintages both wines share, the market prices Rossignol-Trapet at 0.66× Domaine Trapet — but see the correction below: on like-for-like bases it is 0.61×. The framework's capability rungs — A− against A+ — predict 0.55×.

Recomputed on cheapest case per bottle, the market gap is 0.48× across nine vintages against a modelled 0.55× — so the model UNDER-discounts the A+ → A− step, the opposite of what this panel first said. A later ladder on Trapet's own Chapelle ran the same test on the same vineyard and got 0.47× (n=9). Two independent routes within 0.01 of each other; that agreement is the evidence, and the earlier figures had none.

A defect this ladder exposes. The framework gives Rossignol-Trapet higher prestige than Domaine Trapet — A^ against A−^ — while the market charges two-thirds as much for it. Prestige is the price lever, so this is precisely the axis price evidence is entitled to speak to, and it is pointing the wrong way. Both producers sit in the Côte de Nuits calibration panel, so a prestige pass should have caught it; that it has not suggests their cells are too few or too scattered to move the letters. Worth a look independently of any buying decision.

In the glass

Look

Medium ruby, a shade deeper than Trapet's wines — Rossignol-Trapet destems less consistently and the wines carry a little more colour and grip.

⚠ Inferred from house style; not from a critic note.

Nose

Chapelle is the perfumed one: red cherry, violet, a lift of something almost floral. The producer adds a savoury, herbal edge — the framework calls their primary style Spiced & Herbal — so expect dried thyme and pepper behind the fruit.

Palate

Lighter-framed than either Trapet wine and quicker to open. Chapelle sits on shallower soil at the bottom of the grand cru band and gives a wine you can drink at eight years rather than fifteen. Tannins are present but soft.

Palate fit

This is the best stylistic fit of the three ladders, and for a reason worth understanding. On Trapet's Chambertin the producer and site fight to a 35/35 draw. On his Latricières the producer wins at 42%. Here both point the same way — Chapelle-Chambertin is itself a Delicate & Perfumed site, and Rossignol-Trapet carries that as its 40% secondary — so the wine resolves at 52%, the cleanest of the set. There is no tension to resolve.

And the cool years swing hardest here too. 2014 and 2021 both take +3.7, the largest palate-fit gains anywhere on the three ladders. The hot years give back 0.6–0.7. Same lesson as the other two, more pronounced: buy the cool years.

⚠ Note the quality numbers on this page run 81–85, against 87–90 for the Latricières and 91–93 for the Chambertin. That is the A− capability rung doing its work. If you think this wine drinks better than an 81 — and the critics, who score it 92–95, evidently do — then the disagreement is with the capability letter, not with this report.

What the ladder says, and what to distrust

This is a case market, which the other two were not. Nine of the ten cheapest-buyable offers are six-packs — on Trapet's Latricières, six of nine were single bottles. So the prices here are prices you can actually act on, and the outlay column is the real number rather than a taste-test.

The market is steadier at the old end than Trapet's Latricières. Offer counts run 5 to 16 across 2012–2022 with no dead year, where the Latricières collapsed to a single offer at 2013. Rossignol-Trapet makes more wine, sells it cheaper, and more of it stays in circulation instead of being drunk. 2010, 2011 and 2013 were not fetched — the probe showed no dead end, but they add nothing to the producer comparison, which needs the years both ladders share.

Almost every row is dearer than the next case behind it — nine of ten are negative on that measure, worst at 2020 and 2017 (both about 35%). Read as one finding rather than ten: the Hong-Kong-first cheapest-buyable on this wine is systematically not the cheapest case available, and a UK in-bond buyer would do materially better across the board.

What to distrust. First and loudest: r = +0.17 — the model does not track this market, so treat every model-derived figure in the table as indicative only. Second, the 2017's market value (HK$1,113) sits below its own cheapest-buyable (HK$1,672); the pricer flagged it, and it means that particular offer is dear against a pool that is otherwise cheaper — which is why it carries a negative read. Third, the critic column is seven-tenths empty, the same Cru sourcing failure as the other two reports; the three filled entries are marked * because they are wine-searcher's named gate critic rather than a de-duplicated Cru panel. Fourth, the producer-gap number (0.66×) rests on a 0.32–1.00 spread and on the assumption that Chapelle and Latricières are the same rung — it is a much softer reading than the cru comparison that preceded it.
