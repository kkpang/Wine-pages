The anchor search

How a wine-searcher offer list becomes the two numbers the cellar and the framework run on — and which offer is allowed to set the price.

Two numbers, two questions

Cheapest‑buyable
“What would I pay?”

The lowest buyable offer, any format, any size. No corroboration — a listed offer is buyable whether or not a second merchant agrees. By Request is barred: not transactable without an enquiry. Blank if nothing qualifies.

Market value
“What is it worth?”

The anchor offer plus every offer within ±15% of it, averaged. By Request counts here — a merchant quoting the wine is evidence of what it is worth.

Market value below cheapest-buyable is not an error. It means the wine trades cheaper somewhere Ken cannot buy. The gap is information.

Two tests, and they must not be collapsed

TestAsksPasses
reachable()Is this a wholesale market that tells me what the wine is worth?Hong Kong · UK · any designated merchant
buyable()Can I execute this today?HK any offer · UK in-bond or pre-arrival only

A UK retail shop can’t ship to Hong Kong, so its price is barred from the buy price — but it is still evidence of what the wine is worth and keeps its place in the market-value pool. It’s a fact about each offer, not the merchant: 36 of 156 UK merchants sell both ways.

“Hong Kong and the UK” is a proxy, not a geography. Every reachability test asks one thing — can Ken buy here, at a wholesale price? Favourite a good wholesale merchant abroad and it must be named in the code’s allow-list in the same breath.

The sequence

One pass over one pool. There are no stages — that scheme was built and replaced the same day.

  1. Load the wide query — every merchant, not just favourited ones.
  2. If the page rendered the 50-offer cap, load the favourites query and union. On 96% of capped pages the favourites view holds an offer the wide page never showed.
  3. Drop duplicates, merchant families, auctions and junk listings.
  4. Cheapest-buyable is the lowest buyable offer.
  5. Walk up from the cheapest offer, keeping the lowest corroborated offer of each of four types. Stop early on an HK/UK case.
  6. Score each kept offer as price × its type penalty. The lowest score anchors.
  7. Dead if nothing corroborated, or the anchor exceeds 1.5× the cheapest corroborated offer. Otherwise market value is the mean within ±15%.

The four types of offer

This is the heart of it. A penalty is a claim about quality, not price — a loose bottle and a foreign shop each carry risk that a case in Ken’s own market does not, so their price must beat it by the penalty to win.

So a Hong Kong case at 110 beats a Hong Kong bottle at 100 — 110 against 115. At 120 it does not — 120 against 115. And because the walk ascends, the first corroborated offer of a type is the cheapest of that type; an HK/UK case scores at 1.00×, so once one is corroborated nothing above it can win.

This replaced both the old A/B/C/D waterfall and a 15% foreign-preference threshold. Those expressed the same two preferences — format and location — as separate gates that interacted in ways nobody could hold in their head. Here they are one number per offer and a straight minimum. It also reverses an earlier rule that stopped the engine reaching up for a dearer case. The scoring will pay up to 15% for a case, and that is deliberate.

What counts as corroboration

Any other offer within ±15%, of any format and any size. A magnum may second a bottle; a case may second a loose. Nothing seconds it → move up.

A foreign loose needs two seconds, unless one of them is an HK/UK offer, or a case already sits below it.

Why the walk starts at the cheapest offer. It used to start second-lowest, as a guard against a lone cheap outlier setting a price. Corroboration already does that — an outlier fails because nothing sits within 15% of it, not because it was skipped. Starting one offer up meant anchoring on the dearer of any corroborating pair: correcting it moved 86 of 768 cells and every one went down.

When a cell has no price

Two different facts, and worth telling apart.

ReasonShareWhat it means
Nothing corroborated9.6%No two offers agree within 15%. 93% of these pools hold one or two offers — genuinely unreadable markets.
Anchor past 1.5×0.5%Measured against the cheapest corroborated offer. The book really is incoherent.

The floor must be a price something vouches for. Measured against the raw cheapest offer the kill rule fired on 30 cells and 27 were artifacts — Michel Gros × Aux Brûlées 2017 has 41 offers agreeing within 1–2% and died on a single listing 56.8% adrift. An offer nothing corroborates cannot set a price, so it must not set a floor either. Killing rather than keeping is deliberate: a badly-read price pollutes the model, and a missing one does not.

Junk listings

wine-searcher republishes merchant data-entry errors verbatim — Arnoux-Lachaux Clos de Vougeot 2018 at HK$22.76 a bottle, Raveneau Les Clos 2020 at HK$14.42. An offer is dropped when both nothing corroborates it and it sits under 20% of the pool’s median. Genuinely-cheap-but-real offers cluster at 40–90% of median; a 20% cut catches seven offers across four thousand pools and every one is an obscure merchant at an impossible price.

All of it lives in one place — pipeline/anchor_value.py — which is the only thing permitted to produce either number. Every threshold is overridable by environment variable, and every one was set by measurement on identical pools rather than by argument. One rule of thumb earned the hard way: a rule added to one pricing path is a bug until it is added to the other.
