Chambertin is the most powerful site in Gevrey and Trapet is the gentlest grower on it — the wine sits exactly on the line between the two, which is why it suits you better than a Chambertin usually would. The 2017 is the year to take: charming and early-maturing rather than monumental, and it is by far the most liquid row on this ladder — 24 offers and 17 of them cases, where most vintages here show four to eight. That depth is why it is the case to buy rather than the 2013 or 2011, which are cheaper per bottle but available only as single bottles. Avoid the hot years at the top of the price list: 2015, 2018 and 2020 are the three most expensive rows and the only three where the wine scores worse for you than its quality alone would suggest.

In the glass

Look

Pale to medium ruby, and noticeably lighter than a Chambertin from most other addresses — Trapet picks earlier and extracts gently. The older rows here (2010–2013) will have gone towards garnet at the rim.

⚠ Inferred from the house style and the vintages, not from a critic note — colour and texture are rarely described in published reviews.

Nose

Rose petal and red cherry first, then the Gevrey earth underneath — dried herbs, a little woodsmoke, sometimes liquorice. It is perfumed before it is powerful, which is the whole point of this producer.

Palate

Silky rather than firm, with the fruit carried on acidity instead of muscle. The tannin is there — it is Chambertin — but it arrives late and fine-grained. Cool years (2013, 2014, 2021) taste bright and red-fruited; hot years (2015, 2018, 2020) taste denser and less like Trapet.

Palate fit — the real argument

This wine is a tug-of-war, and the producer wins it narrowly. Chambertin as a site is Firm & Structured — the powerful, tannic, slow end of the framework, and on its own that is a style you do not especially enjoy. Trapet is Delicate & Perfumed, which is your best-liked style by some distance. The two land on an exact tie in the model, 35% each, and the resolution goes to the producer only on the tie-break. That asterisk is honest: on a different grower's Chambertin this argument would not work at all.

The year moves the fit more than the wine does — and here that is worth up to 2.8 points. Cool vintages add to the fit and hot ones subtract it. The 2013, 2014 and 2021 all gain +2.2; the 2018 loses 0.6 and the 2015 and 2020 lose about half a point. So the same wine, from the same 1.9 hectares, swings from clearly-yours to not-really-yours purely on the weather. That is the single most useful thing on this page: buy Trapet's cool years.

Your tasting log has one Trapet entry and it is not this domaine — Louis Trapet Chambertin 1981, drunk at a restaurant and marked mixed-negative. That is a different producer, a forty-year-old bottle and one night; it argues nothing about these wines and I have not let it count.

What to buy

1 · 2017 — the case to actually buy

6×75cl · HK$16,835 · HK$2,806/btl · Goedhuis & Co. Hong Kong

For — 23% under the model on quality, 33% under on your taste. Deepest market on the ladder by a distance: 24 offers, 17 of them cases, so you can actually execute and you could execute again later. Charming, early-maturing year that plays to Trapet's gentleness. Drink 2031–2040.

Against — a cheaper case sits behind this one at about 5% less, which will be UK in-bond you would have to handle yourself. 2017 is a very good+ year, not a great one; you are buying the producer and the price, not the vintage.

2 · 2013 — the cheapest way in

1×75cl · HK$2,600 · The Fine Wine Experience

For — the best palate fit on the whole ladder (+2.2, a cool year) and the biggest discount to your taste of any row: 50% under what the model says a wine this well-suited should cost you. Ten case offers exist, so the depth is real.

Against — the cheapest-buyable is a single bottle, and the cases start about 8% higher. You buy by the case, so treat HK$2,600 as the taste test and the case price as the real number. Lightest vintage here; drink 2026–2036, not one to cellar long.

3 · 2019 — if you want the great year

6×75cl · HK$23,440 · HK$3,907/btl · Goedhuis & Co. Hong Kong

For — the best vintage on the ladder that is still cheap: great+, ripe with unusual freshness, Jasper Morris 97. Still 11% under the model on quality and 22% under on taste, and the next case sits 19% above it — the clearest sign on the page that this particular offer is underpriced rather than the market being soft.

Against — HK$23,440 is real money for a wine you will not open before 2033, and its market value rests on a thin pool: one case among only three offers, with two cheaper ones stripped out below the anchor.

What the ladder says, and what to distrust

The market and the model agree on this wine, which is not always true. Correlating the two across all thirteen years gives r = +0.70 — strong, and far better than the +0.43 the Grivault ladder managed. The market's own price spread (2.04×) is wider than the model's (1.46×), which is the reassuring direction: the framework is not claiming to discriminate more finely than buyers actually do. You can trust the percentages on this page, not just the order.

The cheap rows are cheap for a reason, and it is age, not quality. 2011, 2013, 2014 and 2017 are the four least expensive vintages and all four are the ratings the market discounts — very good or very good+ against the great+ and exceptional years. That is the market pricing the vintage chart, and it is exactly the gap you exploit, because Trapet's delicate style needs a cool or moderate year more than it needs a great one.

Eight of the thirteen rows have a cheaper case hiding behind the cheapest-buyable — worst on the 2012, where a case sits 15% below the Hong-Kong-first pick. The pattern is consistent enough to be one finding rather than eight: this wine is systematically cheaper in-bond outside Hong Kong, and if you are willing to handle in-bond yourself the whole ladder is cheaper than it reads here.

What to distrust. The critic column is mostly empty and that is a sourcing failure, not an absence of reviews — Cru's product pages could not be reached for this wine (its search returns nothing and the URL slugs are unguessable), so only the four vintages where wine-searcher named a gate critic outright are filled in. Wine-searcher's own aggregate shows 2 to 8 scores on every vintage here, but it mixes in Jancis Robinson, Gault & Millau and La Revue du Vin, none of which the framework reads — so I have left those out rather than pass them off as evidence. Separately, the 2019's market value rests on a single case among three offers, and the 2022 has no settled case market at all (its cases run 28% over the loose bottles). Both are thin rows; the 2017's 24 offers are worth more than either.
