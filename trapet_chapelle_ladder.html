Trapet · Chapelle-Chambertin

Nine vintages, 2014–2022. The third Trapet grand cru — and the one where the style lands most cleanly on your palate.

producer capability A+ cru level S− cru style Delicate & Perfumed · Rich & Silky 30% producer style Delicate & Perfumed · Spiced & Herbal 30% resolves to Delicate & Perfumed · 70%, the strongest of the four below Latricières on the slope · ~0.6 ha · biodynamic

Read this first — three vintages cost exactly the same. Cru World Wine Hong Kong lists this wine In Bond at a flat HK$1,800 for the 2020, 2021 and 2022 alike, and Y18 at a flat HK$1,963 By Request. These are real offers sitting on each vintage's own page; those two merchants simply do not price this wine by vintage.

That makes the decision unusually easy: at one price, take the best year. The 2022 is great+ and the 2021 only very good, for the same money. It also means the cheapest-buyable column here is partly blind to vintage — so where you want a vintage-sensitive number, read the cheapest case column instead, which ranges 2,487 to 3,348 across those same three years.

Chapelle-Chambertin is the gentlest of the Gevrey grand crus and Trapet is the gentlest grower in the village, so this is the one wine of the four where nothing pulls against anything — it resolves to 70% Delicate & Perfumed, far the cleanest reading on any Trapet ladder. The 2022 is a great+ vintage available at the same flat HK$1,800 as the merely very good 2021, which is the whole argument. Jasper Morris has it at 95–98. If you want a case rather than bottles, the 2017 and 2018 are the two rows sold as genuine six-packs.

In the glass

Look

Pale to medium ruby — the lightest-looking of Trapet's three grand crus, from the shallowest soil of the three sites.

⚠ Inferred from site and house style, not from a critic note.

Nose

The most overtly floral of the set: rose and red cherry, violet, very little of the Gevrey earth that marks the Chambertin. Chapelle is named for a chapel that stood on the plot and the wine has always been the perfumed one.

Palate

Silky and fine-boned, with the tannin arriving late and soft. It drinks earlier than Latricières and much earlier than Chambertin — the framework's window opens 2036 on the 2022, but the older rows here are approachable now.

Palate fit — the best of the four

Nothing pulls against anything on this wine. Chapelle-Chambertin is itself a Delicate & Perfumed site — unusual in Gevrey, where the grand crus are mostly Firm & Structured — and Trapet carries that as its primary style too. The wine resolves at 70%, against 42% on his Latricières and a 35/35 tie on his Chambertin. This is the cleanest stylistic match of anything on these four ladders.

And the vintage swing is the widest yet. 2014 and 2021 both take +4.0 — the largest palate-fit gains recorded on any Trapet wine — while the hot 2015, 2018 and 2020 give back most of it at +0.3. That is a 3.7-point spread from the same vineyard. The year is doing more work than the wine, and more here than anywhere else in the set.

The practical consequence: 2021 is the palate pick and 2022 is the buy, and they cost the same. If you want the wine that suits you most, take the 2021; if you want the better wine at the same price, take the 2022. That is a genuine choice rather than a ranking artefact.

What the ladder says, and what to distrust

The model tracks this market moderately. Against market value across the eight vintages that have one, r = +0.47 — weaker than Trapet's Chambertin (+0.70) and Latricières (+0.60), stronger than Rossignol's Chapelle (+0.17). Treat the percentages as indicative rather than precise.

The ladder splits into two markets and you should choose which one you are in. The 2019 through 2022 are sold as single bottles In Bond, three of them at a flat price; the 2017 and 2018 are sold as proper cases at 2,129–2,254 a bottle. The 2014–2016 sit in between as single bottles from one merchant. Bottles are cheaper here, cases are more expensive, which is the reverse of the usual pattern and is worth knowing before you decide the format.

The cheap end is cheap because of the flat pricing, not because the wine is unloved. The cheapest-case column tells the truer vintage story: 2,179 for the 2017 up to 3,348 for the 2021. On that basis the recent vintages are the dearest, not the cheapest — the opposite of what the headline column shows.

What to distrust. The 2014 has no computable market value — fewer than two case offers — so its row carries a cheapest-buyable and nothing else. The 2015 rests on five offers, the thinnest year here. And the critic column is five-ninths empty, the same Cru sourcing failure as the other three reports: only the vintages where wine-searcher named a gate critic outright are filled, and off-gate scores are deliberately not shown even though every row has some.
